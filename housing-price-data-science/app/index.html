The question

To what extent can machine-learning models predict residential property prices using publicly available structural and geographic housing data — and which of those characteristics matter most? This site walks through the data, the models tested, and an interactive calculator built on the results.

The data

Census block groups across California: income, room counts, population, age of housing stock, and distance-to-ocean category, paired with median home value.

The method

A mean baseline, linear regression, random forest, and gradient boosting were each trained on 80% of the data and scored on a held-out 20% they never saw.

The finding

Income was the single strongest predictor, but location — captured two different ways — added real explanatory power on its own.

How the models were evaluated

Split

80/20 train/test, stratified by income bracket so both sets have similar income distributions.

No leakage

Scaling and encoding were fit only on training data, then applied to the untouched test set.

Metrics

MAE (average dollar error), RMSE (penalizes large misses more), and R² (variance explained).

Research notes

The methodology behind the numbers on this site.

Data source

1990 U.S. Census data at the block-group level for California, widely distributed for teaching and originally sourced from StatLib. 20,640 rows, 10 original fields.

Cleaning decisions

total_bedrooms was missing for 207 rows (~1%) and imputed with the median within each ocean-proximity group. Three engineered ratio features were clipped at the 99.5th percentile to limit the influence of divide-by-small-household outliers.

Limitations

  • Top-coding. Median home values were capped at $500,001 in the original extract (965 rows, ~5%). Those rows were excluded from model training and evaluation so the model wasn't taught a false ceiling — meaning results here don't describe the highest-value block groups well.
  • Age of the data. This is 1990 census data. Relationships between income, location, and price in today's market may differ substantially.
  • Correlation, not causation. A strong association between a feature and price does not mean that feature causes price differences — many are proxies for factors the data doesn't capture directly (school quality, local zoning, amenities).
  • Coarse geography. Each row is a block group average, not an individual home, so the model estimates typical values for an area, not any specific property.
